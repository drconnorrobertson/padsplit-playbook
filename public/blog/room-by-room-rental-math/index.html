Room-by-Room Rental Math: Compare Collected Cash and Full Costs

Published May 5, 2026 · Revised November 21, 2026 · By Dr. Connor Robertson

Compare the cash bridge, not a revenue multiple

Room-by-room renting changes both receipts and responsibilities. More potential rent does not establish a better investment when collections, turnover, operating costs, conversion cash and owner work also change. The three cases here are fictional teaching examples, not observed results, market rents, a platform quote or a property approval.

First confirm permitted use, layout, occupancy limits, approvals and insurance. Five rooms are assumed permitted solely for the arithmetic. An unsupported extra bedroom should not enter an underwriting model because a spreadsheet needs its revenue.

State the period and collection basis

The traditional case assumes $1,800 potential monthly rent and 92% collection, giving $19,872 collected over twelve modeled months. Its fictional manager charges 8% of collected rent. Shared-housing cases assume five rooms at a $195 host base weekly rate for a 52-week planning period: $50,700 potential receipts. Collection is 88% in the base case and 70% in the downside. Those shares combine vacancy and uncollected amounts rather than deducting the same shortfall twice.

The $195 host base rate is not a member’s total bill. No promotions, refunds, move-in receipts or other income are modeled. One-twelfth of the planning-period result is a monthly equivalent, not a forecast of any actual month’s billings or payout.

Trace the booking and service charges

PadSplit’s published host help pages describe a ten-day booking charge and an 8% continuing service charge. Its payout explanation applies that service charge after booking fees. Use the current agreement and settled-payment ledger: transfers, waivers, short stays and promotions can change the calculation. A flat 12% allowance does not describe this example’s fee mechanics.

The base case assumes six new occupancies charged for ten paid days each at the same $195 weekly base rate. No waiver or within-portfolio transfer is assumed. Modeled booking charges are 6 × $195 ÷ 7 × 10, rounded in aggregate to $1,671.43. The service charge is 8% × ($44,616 − $1,671.43), rounded to $3,435.57. Cash after both charges is $39,509. This is an illustrative rounding convention, not a claim about the platform’s transaction-level rounding.

Sources checked November 21, 2026: PadSplit host fee model, monthly payout calculation and earnings and payout FAQs. These sources support fee mechanics, not this example’s rents or outcomes.

Separate outflows, reserve allocations and noncash expense

All cases include $3,600 tax and insurance and $2,400 repairs. Shared-housing cases add $5,100 utilities, $960 internet, $4,500 cleaning and $3,600 local management. Local management is separate from the platform service charge. These are assigned teaching values, not contractor quotes or market averages.

A separate $1,200 replacement-reserve allocation follows those operating outflows. Setting cash aside is different from paying a repair or recording noncash furniture amortization. Reconcile a later replacement funded from that reserve rather than counting the transfer and purchase as unrelated expenses. The result is labeled cash remaining after listed outflows and reserve allocation, before debt and tax—not standardized NOI, spendable profit or a tax deduction.

The traditional case leaves $11,082.24, or $923.52 per modeled month. The shared base leaves $18,149, or $1,512.42 per modeled month. Neither result includes financing or the extra conversion budget.

Let the downside change the conclusion

The combined downside keeps five rooms and the $195 weekly base rate but lowers collection to 70% and doubles full-charge new occupancies to twelve. Booking charges become $3,342.86 and the continuing service charge $2,571.77. Cash after those charges is $29,575.37. Other outflows and the reserve allocation stay fixed.

The downside leaves $8,215.37, or $684.61 per modeled month, below the traditional case. Both collection and turnover changed, so this is a combined stress test, not an isolated estimate of either variable’s effect. It shows why a categorical claim that operating costs cannot close the gross-rent gap is too broad.

Keep conversion capital outside the recurring comparison

Assume an additional $25,000 conversion and launch budget for shared housing only. Keep that one-time cash requirement visible and separate from recurring operations. This illustration does not calculate cash-on-cash return: financing, transaction costs, setup spending and retained liquidity need a consistent capital bridge first.

Debt service, income taxes, sale costs and the owner’s time are excluded. Replace each teaching input with a dated source, retain the collection and fee definitions, and coordinate the model with actual property permissions and operating constraints. A favorable base case is a scenario to investigate, not permission to proceed or a promised outcome.
